The FCC five year rule and the new economics of orbital debris mitigation

For twenty years, orbital debris mitigation was a guideline. Between 2022 and 2026 it became a rule with an enforcement record, an insurance question, and a procurement requirement. That shift is what creates a market for priced debris removal.

From 25 years to five

The long-standing international guideline for post-mission disposal in low Earth orbit was 25 years: a satellite should reenter or be moved to a disposal orbit within 25 years of the end of its mission. The guideline dated from an era of a few hundred active satellites, it was voluntary in most jurisdictions, and compliance was poor. Industry studies through the 2010s found that a large share of LEO payloads were not meeting it.

In September 2022 the Federal Communications Commission adopted a rule shortening that window to five years for satellites operating below 2,000 km that are licensed by, or seek market access through, the FCC. Because the FCC licenses spectrum for nearly every commercial satellite that serves the United States market, the rule's reach extends well beyond American operators. The rule applies to satellites launched after a two year transition that ended in September 2024.

The first enforcement action

Rules change behavior when they are enforced. In October 2023 the FCC announced its first ever space debris enforcement action, a settlement with DISH Network over a geostationary satellite, EchoStar-7, that the operator had failed to move to the disposal orbit specified in its license because it ran short of propellant. The penalty was small, 150,000 dollars, but the precedent was not. The agency established that a disposal commitment in a license is an enforceable obligation and that it will act when one is missed. Every operator's regulatory counsel took note.

ESA, the Zero Debris approach, and the 90 percent requirement

The European Space Agency moved in parallel. ESA's updated space debris mitigation requirements call for a probability of successful disposal of at least 90 percent, a five year post-mission disposal window for LEO, and design features that make a failed satellite easier to remove. ESA has also pushed its Zero Debris approach, with a target that its own missions generate no new debris by 2030, and has invited industry to sign a Zero Debris Charter. Several national regulators and large operators have aligned with the five year window as well, which is turning it into a de facto global standard for new LEO systems.

Insurers have started asking

The space insurance market absorbed heavy losses in 2023 and has been more selective since. Underwriters now routinely ask about disposal reliability, propellant margins, and conjunction procedures before writing policies, and some are pricing debris risk into premiums for crowded altitude bands. For an operator, a credible disposal plan has become part of the cost of capital, not just a regulatory checkbox.

What mitigation does and does not do

All of the above applies to new satellites. It reduces the rate at which new derelicts accumulate, and it is necessary. It does nothing about the objects already in orbit that were launched under the 25 year guideline or before any guideline existed: the heavy rocket bodies and dead satellites that dominate the collision risk in the most crowded planes. Modeling consistently shows that even with perfect compliance on new launches, the existing population in those bands keeps growing through collisions unless the largest objects are actively removed. The Kessler syndrome post covers that research in detail.

Why compliance pressure creates a removal market

Here is the economic consequence. An operator who must guarantee 90 percent disposal reliability has a problem when a satellite fails in a way that prevents disposal. Under the old regime, that was a shrug. Under the new regime, it is a license violation, an insurance event, and a reputational issue with regulators the operator depends on for the next constellation approval. The operator would pay to have the object removed, if removal were available at a price that looked like an insurance premium rather than a space agency program.

The same logic applies to anyone with a large investment in a specific orbital plane. Several gigawatt-scale orbital data center constellations have filed for sun synchronous orbit, the same band that holds the Fengyun-1C fragment cloud, Envisat, and other heavy legacy derelicts. An operator putting billions into that plane has a direct financial interest in the largest collision risks being removed before and during deployment, and a direct interest in a standing removal capability in the plane afterward.

None of this creates demand for a 90 million dollar removal. All of it creates demand for a 3 to 8 million dollar one. That is the price point a reusable servicer reaches by spreading its vehicle, launch, and operations cost over dozens of removals, which is what the Standard SpaceWorX Terrier is designed to do.

What to watch next

  • FCC rulemaking on orbital debris and in-space servicing. The agency has an open proceeding modernizing its rules for servicing, assembly, and manufacturing missions, which will shape how removal services are licensed.
  • Further enforcement. A second and third action, particularly against a LEO operator, will confirm that the five year rule is being policed.
  • Procurement language. Government buyers in the United States and Europe are beginning to write removal readiness and disposal reliability into contracts. That language is where a removal market becomes a line of business.
  • Insurance terms. Watch for explicit debris compliance conditions in policy language, which turn removal into a cost-avoidance decision an operator's finance team can evaluate.
