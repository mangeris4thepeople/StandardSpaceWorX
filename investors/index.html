Investors

A Colorado space startup building the reusable debris removal fleet

Standard SpaceWorX is an early stage aerospace venture based in Loveland, Colorado. This page covers where the company stands, what has been built, the roadmap, and how to reach the founder. It is written for institutional and strategic investors who already know the on-orbit servicing market and want the specifics.

The thesis in one paragraph

Active debris removal has absorbed more than a billion dollars of program funding without removing a single uncooperative legacy object, because every vehicle built so far is consumed by its first job. A reusable servicer that performs dozens of removals per tank of propellant moves the cost per object from the 90 million dollar class toward the 3 to 8 million dollar class. At that price, removal becomes a compliance and insurance line item that satellite operators, insurers, and governments will pay for repeatedly. The same vehicle earns Earth observation and hosted payload revenue between removals, so the fleet generates revenue before the removal market is fully priced. Standard SpaceWorX is building that vehicle, the Terrier, and the plane-locked fleet model around it.

Stage

Standard SpaceWorX is pre-seed. The company operates as a venture of Standard Data Company, a Loveland, Colorado S corporation that holds all Standard SpaceWorX intellectual property. We are raising to fund the ground prototype of the Terrier capture system and the engineering team to build it. There is no outside capital in the company today.

What has been built

  • Mission architecture. The plane-locked, reusable servicer model, the three priority campaign planes, and the three revenue stream structure described across this site.
  • Satellite design reference package. A reference design for the Terrier vehicle covering orbit, propulsion sizing, mission cycle, and the 45 missions per tank reuse baseline.
  • Patent landscape study. A review of more than forty capture, servicing, and debris removal patents mapping what is claimed, what has expired, and where the open design space for a standardized capture mechanism sits.
  • Corporate structure and brand. The venture, its IP ownership, its public presence, and its investor and partner channels.

Why the window is 2027 to 2030

Regulation now has enforcement behind it: the FCC five year disposal rule is in force and the first fine has been issued, and ESA requires a 90 percent probability of successful disposal. Insurers have started asking about disposal before writing policies. Multiple large constellations, including gigawatt-scale orbital data center filings, target the same sun synchronous band where the heaviest legacy debris sits, which gives well-capitalized operators a direct reason to pay for clearing it. And next generation launch pricing makes a standardized servicer fleet affordable to deploy for the first time. A company that has a working capture system and a vehicle in the plane when that demand arrives is positioned to take the market. One that starts then is not.

Use of proceeds

The current raise funds the Phase 1 ground prototype: the capture mechanism, the test rig for representative tumbling targets, the guidance and navigation simulation, and the core engineering hires to run the program. Investors in diligence receive the detailed budget, hiring plan, and the design reference package.

Why Colorado

Colorado has one of the largest aerospace economies in the United States, anchored by Space Force installations in Colorado Springs and Aurora, prime contractors and satellite manufacturers along the Front Range, and university programs at Boulder and Fort Collins that produce spacecraft engineers every year. Loveland sits in Northern Colorado, inside commuting range of Fort Collins and Boulder and within reach of the Denver aerospace cluster. For a company whose first hires are propulsion, GNC, and mechanisms engineers, this is the right labor market, and it is a domestic one, which matters to government and defense buyers.
