The Terrier: a reusable servicing satellite built for capture

The Terrier is the Standard SpaceWorX removal vehicle. It is a reusable servicing satellite that lives in one orbital plane, phases to a debris target, captures it, disposes of it, and returns to a parked orbit to wait for the next job.

Why we call it a Terrier

Terriers were bred to go into the ground after something that does not want to be caught, bring it out, and go back in. That is the job. A Terrier satellite is small relative to the objects it hunts, it is persistent, and it works a defined territory rather than roaming. The name also signals something about the design philosophy: this is a working vehicle, not a one-off science demonstration.

The problem with single-use removal vehicles

Every active debris removal mission flown or contracted to date follows the same pattern. A chaser spacecraft launches, rendezvous with one target, attaches to it, and then deorbits with it. The chaser is consumed. The cost of the entire vehicle, its launch, and its operations team is charged to one object. That is how a single removal ends up in the 90 million dollar class, and it is why, after more than a billion dollars committed across the industry, no uncooperative legacy debris object has yet been removed from orbit.

At that price, removal is a demonstration budget line, not a service. The published models for stabilizing low Earth orbit call for removing five to ten large objects per year, every year, for decades. Single-use vehicles cannot do that at any budget a customer will pay.

How the Terrier changes the math

The Terrier separates the expensive part of the system from the consumable part. The vehicle, its propulsion, its guidance and navigation stack, and its capture mechanism are reused across many targets. The only thing consumed per mission is propellant and a small amount of hardware life. Spread the fixed cost over dozens of jobs and the cost per object moves toward the 3 to 8 million dollar class, which is a number operators, insurers, and governments can budget against.

The mission cycle

  1. Park. The Terrier sits dormant at 865 km in its home plane, drawing minimal power. The downward facing camera collects Earth observation imagery while it waits.
  2. Wake. Ground operations issue tasking against a specific catalog object in the same plane. The vehicle brings up its navigation sensors and propulsion.
  3. Phase. The Terrier drops roughly 25 km to a faster orbit and laps forward until it closes on the target, then climbs back to match.
  4. Capture. Close proximity operations, characterization of the target's tumble, and a mechanical grapple. This is the hardest step and the one the ground prototype program is focused on.
  5. Dispose. Depending on the object and the customer, the Terrier provides a deorbit assist to a short-lived altitude, boosts the object to a disposal orbit, or hands it to a third party disposal vehicle.
  6. Return. The Terrier releases, climbs back to the parking altitude, and goes dormant again. Then it does the whole thing again, up to 45 times per tank.

Satellite capture: the engineering focus

Rendezvous and proximity operations are well understood. Capture of an uncooperative, tumbling object is not routine, and it is where single-use programs have spent most of their money and schedule. The Terrier approach is to standardize the problem rather than solve each target as a bespoke mission. A finite set of target classes, a finite set of approach geometries, and a capture mechanism designed for the common features of rocket bodies and large derelicts, rather than one custom interface per object.

Standard SpaceWorX has completed a patent landscape study covering more than forty capture, servicing, and removal patents to map what is already claimed, what has expired, and where the open design space is. The engineering prototype program begins with the capture mechanism on the ground, under representative lighting and motion, before anything flies.

What a Terrier does between jobs

A debris removal vehicle that only earns when it is removing debris is idle most of its life. The Terrier is designed so that idle time earns. The home plane is sun synchronous, which means the same ground lighting on every pass, the orbit commercial imaging companies pay the most for. The Terrier's own camera collects imagery during dormant periods. Spare mass, power, and pointing budget on the vehicle is leased to hosted communications payloads, transmit and receive hardware only. Those two revenue streams run whether or not a removal contract is active, which is what makes a Terrier fleet financeable before the removal market is fully priced.

The first campaign

The first Terrier campaign targets the 98 degree sun synchronous plane between 700 and 1,100 km. That plane holds the Fengyun-1C fragment cloud, Envisat, and several heavy upper stages, and it is the same band that large orbital data center constellations have filed to occupy. It is the highest value plane to clear and the most valuable plane to be parked in afterward. Subsequent campaigns move to the 71 degree SL-16 cluster at 840 km and the SL-8 fleets between 74 and 83 degrees.

Who the Terrier program serves

  • Satellite operators who need a specific object moved out of their shell, or who need a disposal partner to meet regulatory requirements.
  • Government and defense buyers with catalog objects of concern and a need for a domestic, repeatable removal capability.
  • Aerospace partners in launch, propulsion, sensors, and ground operations who want to be on the vehicle.
