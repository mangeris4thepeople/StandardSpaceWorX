The work

One vehicle. Three revenue streams.

A Terrier lives in orbit, works a route, and earns in three ways while it does. Reuse is what turns active debris removal from a grant-funded demonstration into a business.

REVENUE 01

Active debris removal

Each Terrier is assigned to one orbital plane, the way a crew is assigned to one job site. It parks dormant, wakes on tasking, phases to the target, captures it, disposes of it, and returns.

Reusing one vehicle across dozens of targets moves the cost per object from the industry's 90 million dollar class toward the 3 to 8 million dollar class.

REVENUE 02

Earth observation data

The home plane is sun synchronous orbit, the most valuable imaging orbit in space. Between jobs the Terrier's camera points down and collects imagery for sale.

The camera is ours alone. We do not sell imaging or recording space on the vehicle; the data business stays in house.

REVENUE 03

Hosted communications payloads

Spare mass, power, and pointing on each Terrier is leased to communications tenants, the way carriers lease space on a cell tower.

Transmit and receive hardware only. No cameras, no recorders, no exceptions.

Why now

The 2027 to 2030 convergence

Regulation with teeth

The FCC five year disposal rule is in force and the first debris fine has been levied. ESA requires 90 percent disposal success. Insurers now ask for debris compliance before writing policies.

Tenants are moving in

Gigawatt scale orbital data center constellations have filed for sun synchronous orbit, the same band the worst debris contaminates. Somebody has to clear the site before they move in.

Launch costs are falling

Next generation launch pricing makes a standardized, mass produced servicer fleet affordable for the first time. Rideshare to our first plane is already routine.
