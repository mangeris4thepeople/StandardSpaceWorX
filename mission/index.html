Mission

Active debris removal only works if the vehicle comes back

The mission of Standard SpaceWorX is to make active debris removal a repeatable, priced service rather than a series of one-off demonstrations. The way to do that is to stop throwing the vehicle away.

The state of the problem

Low Earth orbit holds tens of thousands of tracked objects larger than ten centimeters and far more that are too small to track but large enough to end a mission. Most of the mass is not in small fragments. It is in a few thousand large derelicts: dead satellites and spent upper stages that have been in orbit for decades and will stay there for centuries. When two of them collide, the result is not two pieces of debris but thousands, and each of those becomes a new collision risk. That feedback loop is the Kessler syndrome, and the concentration of heavy objects in a handful of orbital planes is what makes it a near-term concern rather than a theoretical one.

The research consensus on what it takes to stabilize the environment has been stable for more than a decade: remove on the order of five to ten of the largest, highest-risk objects every year, prioritized by mass and collision probability, in addition to the post-mission disposal rules that now apply to new satellites. Mitigation rules keep the problem from getting worse. Only removal makes it better.

What single-use deorbit actually costs

The debris removal missions that have been flown or contracted so far share an architecture. One chaser spacecraft, one target, one deorbit, and the chaser burns up with the target. Every dollar of vehicle development, launch, and operations is amortized over a single object. The result is programs in the 90 million dollar class per object, funded by space agencies as technology demonstrations.

At that cost, a stabilization campaign of ten objects per year would run close to a billion dollars annually, forever. No customer exists for that. Satellite operators will not pay it, insurers will not underwrite against it, and governments fund it only as research. The architecture is the reason the market has not formed.

There is a second cost that is easier to miss. Each single-use mission is a bespoke engineering program. The capture interface is designed for one target, the approach is planned for one tumble state, and the team disbands or moves on afterward. Nothing compounds. The second mission is nearly as expensive as the first.

Why reusable removal is different

A reusable servicer separates the expensive part of the system, the vehicle, from the consumable part, the propellant. The Standard SpaceWorX Terrier is designed to perform up to 45 removal missions on one tank of propellant. The vehicle cost, the launch cost, and the operations team are spread across all of them. Cost per object moves from the 90 million dollar class toward the 3 to 8 million dollar class, which is the range where a disposal contract looks like an insurance premium rather than a national program.

Reuse also changes the engineering. When one vehicle will capture dozens of objects, it has to be designed for a class of targets rather than one target, and the capture procedure has to be a repeatable playbook rather than a custom plan. That discipline is harder up front and far cheaper over a campaign. It is the same shift that happened when launch went from expendable to reusable: the first reusable vehicle is more expensive to design, and every flight after that is cheaper than anything the old model could offer.

Plane-locked operations

Changing orbital planes is the most expensive maneuver in low Earth orbit. A vehicle that tries to serve every plane spends its propellant getting between jobs rather than doing them. The Terrier concept assigns each vehicle to one plane, the way a construction crew is assigned to one job site, and works that plane until it is clear. The three priority planes, the 98 degree sun synchronous band, the 71 degree SL-16 cluster, and the 74 to 83 degree SL-8 fleets, together hold an estimated 80 percent of the mass that matters. Three plane-locked campaigns address most of the problem.

A plane-locked vehicle has another advantage. When it is not removing debris, it is still in a useful orbit. In sun synchronous orbit that means it can collect Earth observation imagery and host communications payloads between jobs. The vehicle earns while it waits, which is what makes a removal fleet financeable before the removal market is fully priced.

Removal is becoming a compliance requirement

Three things changed between 2022 and 2026 that turn debris removal from a public good into a purchasable service. First, regulators now enforce disposal. The FCC's five year post-mission disposal rule is in effect and the agency has issued its first enforcement action against an operator for a failed disposal. ESA's mitigation requirements call for a 90 percent probability of successful disposal. Second, insurers have started asking about disposal plans before writing policies. Third, new large constellations, including gigawatt-scale orbital data center proposals, have filed for the exact sun synchronous band where the heaviest legacy debris sits. Operators with billions committed to a plane have a direct interest in clearing it.

None of these developments create demand for a 90 million dollar removal. All of them create demand for a 3 to 8 million dollar one.

What we are building toward

  1. A ground-proven capture system. The hardest step in the cycle is grappling a tumbling, uncooperative object. The Terrier program starts there, on the ground, before anything flies.
  2. A first Terrier in the 98 degree plane. One vehicle, one plane, a demonstration on a cooperative target followed by the first commercial removals.
  3. A standing fleet. Terriers parked in each priority plane, tasked by operators, insurers, and governments, earning imaging and hosted payload revenue between removals.
